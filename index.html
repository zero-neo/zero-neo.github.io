About

I love extracting insights from noisy data. Additionally, I'm curious about the noise itself: its sources and consequences.

My PhD at the University of Konstanz tracked down a source. In larval zebrafish, a considerable fraction of what looks like random trial-to-trial variability turned out to be structured by the animal's recent sensory and motor history. My master's thesis, at the Indian Institute of Science, explored a consequence: noise can itself drive small groups of fish to school. Aside from this, I worked on several small projects during my Master's: using IMU data from mobile phones to track falls, developing nucleotide-sequence alignment methods using signal processing, and identifying biomarkers unique to small-cell lung cancer.

Projects

Sensory and response history in larval zebrafish

Larval zebrafish tend to follow moving patterns. But not always. Their recent past exerts two opposing influences: what they've just seen pushes their next turn away, while what they've just done pulls them to repeat it. A model with three timescales of memory predicts a considerable fraction of choices that look random.

Rewiring fish schools

What if fish could only meet each other through a screen? With virtual coupling, each fish swims alone and interacts with virtual counterparts that move as the others do, in real time. Every interaction passes through software, so I can redesign who influences whom, and how. Paper in preparation.

arrowsmith

arrowsmith developed from the analysis I did in the virtual coupling project. Inspired by Katz plots, it turns tracking data into vector fields describing interactions between particles. It has a built-in simulator to check the maps against known rules. Working on: recovering candidate equations for these vector fields.
